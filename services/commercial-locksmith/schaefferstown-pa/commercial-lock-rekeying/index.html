What commercial lock rekeying actually involves

On arrival, the first thing a technician checks is not the key itself — it is the hardware. For commercial lock rekeying in Schaefferstown, that means looking at each cylinder, confirming the lock brand and keyway, and deciding whether the existing hardware can be rekeyed cleanly or whether a worn part will fight the job. A shop around the historic town square may have a mix of Kwikset, Schlage, or higher-security Medeco or Mul-T-Lock cylinders on different doors, and each one needs its own plan. We also look at whether the locks are standard pin tumbler sets, whether they are keyed alike already, and whether any doors have been changed over the years without a record.

Once the hardware is identified, the cylinder is removed, the old pins are cleared out, and new pins are matched to the new key using a pinning kit and cut key. If a door is binding, has a damaged tailpiece, or the mortise body is worn, rekeying can expose problems that were hidden before. That is especially common in older stone buildings near the historic square or on properties along Route 419 and Route 897, where doors may have settled or been repaired more than once. The process is simple in concept but exact in practice: disassemble, decode, pin, test, and confirm that each key turns smoothly and only the intended locks respond.

Good rekeying is about control. After staff turnover, lost keys, or a change in who manages the building, the goal is to restore key control without replacing every lock on the door. That works best when the existing hardware is still solid and the customer can tell us which doors should match, which should stay separate, and whether any master key setup is in play.

Good to know

Common questions.

What does a locksmith check first when rekeying commercial locks?

The first check is the actual hardware: brand, cylinder type, keyway, and condition. A tech needs to know whether the lock is a standard pin tumbler cylinder, a commercial grade mortise lock, or a higher-security setup. Worn parts, damaged springs, or mismatched cores can change the plan before any pins are swapped.

Can you rekey all the locks in a shop near Schaefferstown's town square to one key?

Often, yes — if the cylinders are compatible and the hardware is in good shape. The tech will compare each lock, confirm which doors should be included, and then pin the cylinders to match one master key or one common operating key. Some high-security systems or mixed hardware may need a different approach.

How is commercial rekeying different from replacing the whole lock?

Rekeying keeps the existing lock body and changes the key that operates it. That means the cylinder is opened, old pins are removed, and new pins are installed for a different key cut. Replacement is for hardware that is broken, badly worn, or the wrong grade for the door.

What makes rekeying harder on older buildings in Schaefferstown?

Older buildings around the historic square or on the surrounding farms can have settled doors, mixed hardware, or repaired frames. That can make cylinders bind, keys drag, or deadbolts miss alignment. A locksmith checks the fit, the latch, and the lock body before rekeying so the new key works the way it should.

What should I have ready before calling for commercial lock rekeying?

Have the building's address, a list of every door that needs to change, and any existing keys you still have. If you know the lock brands — such as Schlage, Kwikset, Baldwin, Yale, or Medeco — that helps too. It is also useful to explain whether you want all doors on one key or separate access by room.
