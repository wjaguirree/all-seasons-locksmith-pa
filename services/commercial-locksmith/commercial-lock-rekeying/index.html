What commercial lock rekeying actually involves

When we arrive for commercial lock rekeying, the first thing we check is not the key itself — it's the condition and layout of the locks already on the doors. A storefront latch, office lever, or back-door deadbolt may all use different keyways, different cylinder types, and different levels of wear. We verify the hardware, note whether the locks are Kwikset, Schlage, Baldwin, Yale, Medeco, or Mul-T-Lock, and confirm which doors should stay on one key and which should be separated for access control. That first check tells us whether the job is a straightforward pin change or whether a worn cylinder, damaged tailpiece, or mismatched core needs attention before rekeying can hold up properly.

From there, the process is mechanical and precise. We remove the cylinder or core, read the existing pinning, and repin it to match a new key using a pinning kit and proper key gauges. On higher-security commercial hardware, we may decode the key or origination it from the lock code instead of copying an old key that might be worn. If the building uses restricted or high-security hardware, the key system may involve control keys, master keying, or restricted keyways that need to be kept consistent across doors. What makes the job easier is having the current working key, knowing which employees still need access, and understanding whether the doors are standard Grade 2 hardware or heavier ANSI Grade 1 equipment. What complicates it is stripped set screws, packed dirt in the cylinder, mixed brands, or locks that were already forced. The goal is a clean rekey that keeps the hardware in place and restores control over who can open the doors.

Commercial Rekeying Variants

Commercial rekeying is not one single job. The simplest version is a standard cylinder rekey, where the lock hardware stays on the door and the plug is repinned to a new key. That covers a lot of Schlage, Kwikset, Yale, Arrow, Falcon and similar cylinders on offices, shops, warehouses and common employee entrances throughout Dauphin, Cumberland and Lebanon counties. If the locks are keyed alike, one operating key can run several doors. If they are keyed different, each door or department gets its own key.

Master key rekeying is a different animal. The cylinder has to be pinned so one change key works a specific door while a master key still works the group. That means checking the existing key system, key bittings, pin stacks and any cross-keying before cutting new keys. Done carelessly, a master system can create unwanted key interchange, where one employee key opens a door it should not. Small businesses often need a simple owner key and employee key setup; larger commercial spaces may need separate groups for offices, storage, mechanical rooms and exterior doors.

Some commercial rekeys involve interchangeable core hardware, such as SFIC or LFIC cores found on many institutional and commercial doors. Instead of repinning a conventional cylinder on the door, the core may be removed with a control key and rekeyed or replaced as a core. High-security and restricted keyway systems, including Medeco, Mul-T-Lock and certain Schlage Primus-style systems, add another layer because keys, blanks and authorization rules may be controlled. In those cases, the job is less about making any key fit and more about preserving the integrity of the key system after staff turnover.

Cylinders We Actually Rekey

Commercial rekeying is usually cylinder work, not whole-door replacement. On offices, retail suites, medical spaces, warehouses, churches and municipal-style buildings across Dauphin, Cumberland and Lebanon counties, we commonly work on Schlage, Kwikset, Yale, Arrow, Falcon, Baldwin, Corbin Russwin, Sargent, Medeco and Mul-T-Lock hardware. The actual part being changed is the keying inside the cylinder: bottom pins, master pins when present, springs, tailpieces or cams when needed, and sometimes the plug itself if it is worn or damaged. Mortise cylinders, rim cylinders, key-in-knob cylinders, lever cylinders, deadbolt cylinders, storefront cylinders and removable/interchangeable cores all rekey differently.

A standard commercial lever may take a conventional 5- or 6-pin cylinder. A glass aluminum storefront door may use a mortise cylinder with a cam that has to match the Adams Rite-style lock body. Panic hardware often uses a rim cylinder to control exterior trim while the exit device still has to meet egress requirements. Interchangeable core systems, including small-format cores, are a different animal because the control key, operating key and core chambers all matter. If the former employee has a control key, the risk is bigger than a normal office key.

Restricted and high-security keyways need special care. Medeco and Mul-T-Lock systems may involve angled cuts, sidebar components, telescoping pins or dealer-controlled blanks. Those systems cannot be treated like ordinary Schlage C or Kwikset keyways. A good rekey keeps compatible hardware in service, preserves master-key structure when appropriate, and identifies cylinders that are too worn, mismatched or non-serviceable to trust after turnover.

Rekeying From Door To Key

A commercial rekey starts with control, not with a screwdriver. The technician first confirms who is authorized to request changes, then walks the building with that person to identify every opening affected by the staff change: front entries, employee doors, offices, stockrooms, utility rooms, file rooms, and exterior service doors. Each lock is matched to its hardware type and keyway before anything is taken apart. A Schlage C keyway mortise cylinder, a Kwikset knob cylinder, an Arrow lever, a Falcon interchangeable core, and a Medeco or Mul-T-Lock restricted cylinder are not handled the same way. If the building uses a master key system, the tech also checks which doors are supposed to pass change keys, sub-master keys, and any top master key.

Once the plan is clear, cylinders or cores are removed one at a time and rekeyed on the bench in the van or at a controlled work surface inside the building. The plug is taken out, the old pins are dumped, and the cylinder is repinned to the new cut depths using the proper pinning kit. Master-pinned cylinders are checked carefully so the new employee key works only where intended while approved master keys still operate the correct doors. On interchangeable core systems, the control key function is tested separately from the operating key.

Before the job is buttoned up, every rekeyed lock is tested from both sides of the door with the new key, any retained master keys, and no old keys that should have been removed from service. The technician checks for rough rotation, worn plugs, loose set screws, bad tailpieces, misaligned strikes, latch drag, and exit hardware that could affect legal egress under ADA expectations and NFPA 101 exit-device rules. Keys are then grouped and identified so management knows exactly what opens what.

Why Rekeys Get Complicated

A straightforward commercial rekey is usually a standard pin tumbler cylinder with a known operating key, clean hardware, and no master-key system to preserve. A typical Schlage C, Kwikset KW1, Yale, Arrow, Falcon, or similar conventional keyway can often be removed, repinned with the right pinning kit, tested at the door, and put back into service without changing the lock body. The work is cleaner when the door closes properly, the cylinder set screw is not stripped, and management can say exactly which doors should work on which keys.

The difficult cases start when the lock is only the visible part of a larger key system. A building may have a master key, change keys for tenant suites, storage rooms, offices, mechanical rooms, and a grand master held by ownership. Rekeying after staff turnover without breaking that hierarchy takes careful key bitting, pin stack math, and verification at each cylinder. Small format interchangeable core systems, common on commercial properties, add another layer because the control key must operate correctly or the core may not come out cleanly. Restricted keyways, Medeco, Mul-T-Lock, and older high-security cylinders can limit what can be duplicated or rekeyed in the field.

Condition matters too. A cylinder packed with graphite, paint, corrosion, bent keys, or worn plug chambers may technically rekey but still operate poorly afterward. Glass storefront locks, mortise cylinders, rim cylinders, panic hardware trim, and lever sets all come apart differently. Across Dauphin, Cumberland, and Lebanon counties, the hardest jobs are not always the biggest buildings; they are the ones with missing keys, unknown master systems, mixed hardware brands, and no record of who was supposed to have access in the first place.

Prepare the Rekey List

Before the van rolls, decide exactly which openings are in the rekey. Walk the building and count exterior doors, office doors, stock rooms, file rooms, mechanical rooms, padlocks, gates and any interior doors that should stay keyed different. Note the hardware type if you can: cylindrical knob or lever, deadbolt, mortise cylinder, rim cylinder on an exit device, storefront Adams Rite-style hardware, or interchangeable core. Brand names help too: Schlage, Kwikset, Yale, Arrow, Falcon, Sargent, Medeco, Mul-T-Lock and Best-style SFIC all change the parts and key blanks we bring.

Have someone on site with authority to make keying decisions. Staff turnover rekeys often involve more than making one old key stop working. Decide who gets which key, whether managers need a master key, whether certain rooms should be keyed alike, and whether any existing master system must be preserved. If you use restricted keys, gather the key control card or authorization paperwork. If there are several buildings across Dauphin, Cumberland or Lebanon counties, label keys and doors clearly so the system does not get crossed up.

Expect the technician to verify access, remove cylinders where needed, repin them with a pinning kit, cut and test new keys, and check that latches and exit devices still operate correctly. Unlock doors beforehand when possible, but do not disable closers, prop fire doors as a habit, or alter panic hardware. Point out sticky locks, missing keys, broken levers and doors that drag; those issues can affect whether a clean rekey is enough or whether a repair should be handled before the new keys are issued.

Good to know

Common questions.

What does a commercial locksmith check first when rekeying office locks?

The first check is the hardware itself: brand, keyway, cylinder type, and condition. A technician needs to know whether the locks are standard pin-tumbler cylinders, interchangeable cores, or high-security hardware. Wear, stripped screws, and damaged cylinders can change the plan before any pins are moved. That inspection tells us whether the locks can be rekeyed cleanly or need repair first.

Can all the locks in my building be rekeyed to one key?

Often yes, but only if the lock family and keyway allow it. A building with matching Schlage or Kwikset cylinders is usually simpler than one with mixed brands or older replacement hardware. Interior offices, entries, and storage rooms can sometimes be set up under one key or a master key system, depending on who needs access and how the hardware is arranged.

Is rekeying better than replacing commercial locks after staff turnover?

If the lock bodies are still in good shape, rekeying is usually the more direct fix because it changes which keys work without changing every door set. Replacement makes more sense when the cylinder is worn, the hardware is damaged, or the lock no longer matches the rest of the building. A good inspection tells you which route protects access control best.

What kind of keys or lock brands can be rekeyed on commercial doors?

Many commercial cylinders from Schlage, Kwikset, Baldwin, Yale, Medeco, and Mul-T-Lock can be rekeyed, though the method depends on the keyway and security level. Standard pin-tumbler cylinders are straightforward. High-security systems may require decoding, restricted blanks, or control-key procedures. The technician needs to identify the exact hardware before setting new pins.

What should I have ready before calling for commercial lock rekeying in Central Pennsylvania?

Have a working key if one still exists, and know which doors need to stay on the same key and which should be separated. It also helps to know the rough lock types on the building, such as lever locks, deadbolts, or interchangeable-core hardware. If you're in Dauphin, Cumberland, or Lebanon county, call (223) 240-3505 and be ready to describe the doors and the access you want to keep.

Can every commercial lock be rekeyed?

Most standard commercial cylinders can be rekeyed if the hardware is in serviceable condition. That includes many Schlage, Kwikset, Yale, Arrow and Falcon knob, lever, deadbolt, mortise and rim cylinders. Rekeying will not fix a worn latch, damaged tailpiece, broken cam, loose lever body or badly corroded cylinder. In those cases, cylinder or lock replacement may be the better repair.

How does rekeying affect a master key system?

A commercial master key system has to be changed carefully, not guessed at cylinder by cylinder. The locksmith needs to know which keys should operate each door, which keys should stop working, and whether a grand master or department master is involved. If a master key is lost, more cylinders may need changes because that key can operate multiple doors by design.

What if our building uses interchangeable cores?

Interchangeable core systems, including small format cores used by brands like BEST, Arrow and Falcon, are handled differently than standard cylinders. If the control key is available, cores can be removed cleanly and rekeyed at the bench or swapped as needed. If the control key is missing, the work becomes more technical and may involve decoding, picking or other removal methods before the new keying can be set.

Can restricted or high-security keys be rekeyed?

Restricted and high-security systems such as Medeco, Mul-T-Lock and certain controlled Schlage or Yale keyways are not the same as open keyway locks. Rekeying may require authorization, matching key blanks, factory-controlled components or a registered key system record. Some cylinders can be repinned, while others are better handled by replacing cores within the same authorized system so key control is preserved.
