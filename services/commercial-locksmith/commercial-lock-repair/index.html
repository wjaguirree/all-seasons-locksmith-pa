What commercial lock repair actually involves

Commercial lock repair sounds simple until you're standing in front of a door that has to close, latch, and reopen all day without fail. Some jobs are straightforward: a worn cylinder, a loose lever set, a misaligned strike, or a closer that has drifted out of adjustment. Others get complicated fast when the door itself is sagging, the latch bolt is damaged, the deadlatch won't engage, or the hardware is mixed—say a Schlage lever on an ANSI Grade 1 mortise body with a separate panic device and door closer. The first step is always to inspect the full opening, not just the lock. A repair that ignores hinge wear, frame movement, or latch prep usually comes back as the same problem.

From there, a locksmith checks how the mechanism is failing: key not turning smoothly, latch not throwing fully, lever hanging loose, cylinder spinning, or the door binding before the lock even gets involved. Depending on the hardware, that may mean re-pinning a cylinder, replacing worn springs, tightening through-bolts, decoding a keyway, or resetting the strike so the latch enters cleanly. On commercial jobs, we also look at how traffic hits the door—deliveries, employees, customers, weather exposure, and slamming all change the wear pattern. In Harrisburg-area buildings across Dauphin, Cumberland, and Lebanon counties, the hardest repairs are often the ones where the lock is only part of the story and the opening needs to be brought back into alignment as a system.

Repairs are easier when the original key works at least part of the time, the brand and function are known, and the door hardware is still intact. They get harder when parts are bent, stripped, painted over, or previously forced. When we can repair the existing lock instead of replacing the whole set, that often keeps the opening closer to its original setup and gets it working the way it should.

Commercial Lock Repair Types

Commercial lock repair is not one job. A storefront mortise lock on an aluminum glass door fails differently than a cylindrical lever on an office suite, and both are different from an exit device on a rear delivery door. Mortise bodies often need case work: worn hub springs, loose set screws, bent cam parts, or a cylinder tailpiece that is not driving the latch correctly. Cylindrical lever repairs are more often about through-bolt alignment, latch wear, stripped mounting posts, or a lever clutch that no longer returns cleanly. On rim and vertical-rod exit devices, the repair may involve dogging assemblies, strike alignment, latchbolt throw, rod adjustment, or worn head mechanism parts, with NFPA 101 and ADA egress requirements always in the background.

Keyed-cylinder problems are their own category. A Schlage, Yale, Arrow, Falcon, Kwikset, Baldwin, Medeco, or Mul-T-Lock cylinder may turn poorly because of worn pins, a damaged plug, a bad key cut, debris in the keyway, or years of dry graphite and oil packed together. Repair can mean cleaning, repinning, replacing springs, correcting a cam or tailpiece, or swapping only the cylinder while keeping the existing lock body. Restricted and high-security keyways require more care because not every cylinder can be casually substituted without changing the key system.

Door-position problems can look like lock failure but are really frame, hinge, closer, or strike issues. In commercial buildings across Dauphin, Cumberland, and Lebanon counties, high-traffic doors often sag, slam, swell, or get hit by carts. The practical repair is diagnosing whether the lock is broken, misaligned, or being forced to do a door-control job it was never built to do.

Commercial Hardware We Repair

Commercial lock repair is not one kind of lock. On a typical service call in Dauphin, Cumberland, or Lebanon County, the problem may be inside a Grade 1 mortise lock on a glass aluminum storefront door, a cylindrical lever lock on an office suite, or a rim panic device on a rear exit. We work on Schlage, Yale, Sargent, Corbin Russwin, Arrow, Falcon, Marks, Adams Rite, Von Duprin, Detex, and similar commercial hardware. The failure may be a worn latchbolt, broken tailpiece, loose thru-bolts, stripped mounting posts, misaligned strike, failing dogging assembly, bent exit-device rail, or a cylinder that has been damaged by dirt, bad keys, or heavy use.

Storefront doors have their own issues. Adams Rite-style deadlatches, hook bolts, paddle handles, and narrow-stile cylinders depend on tight door alignment, so a lock problem can actually be a closer, hinge, pivot, or threshold problem showing up at the latch. Wood and hollow-metal doors usually involve mortise cases, cylindrical chassis locks, deadbolts, electric strikes, latch guards, continuous hinges, door closers, and panic bars. We repair key-in-lever cylinders, interchangeable core systems, small-format IC cores, mortise cylinders, rim cylinders, and restricted keyway cylinders where the hardware can be serviced without defeating the key control system.

The work is mechanical first: find the bind, check the strike, test latch throw, inspect the spindle and cam, tighten what has loosened, replace the failed wear part, and reassemble it so the door latches consistently. When hardware is cracked, obsolete, or below the duty level the opening needs, repair may not be the right answer.

Commercial Repair Work Sequence

A commercial lock repair starts at the door, not at the cylinder. The technician first checks how the opening is being used: storefront aluminum, hollow metal, wood, interior office, restroom, stockroom, or exterior service door. Then the door is tested open and closed to separate a lock problem from a door problem. We look for hinge sag, frame movement, loose continuous hinges, rubbing weatherstrip, latch misalignment, worn strikes, closer sweep issues, and panic or lever trim that is binding under load. If the latch works with the door open but drags when closed, replacing parts without correcting alignment will not hold up.

Once the cause is narrowed down, the lock is taken apart far enough to inspect the working pieces. On lever sets, mortise locks, deadlatches, exit device trim, and cylindrical locks from brands such as Schlage, Yale, Falcon, Arrow, Sargent, Kwikset, and Baldwin, we check spindle wear, tailpieces, set screws, springs, latchbolts, faceplates, cam timing, and cylinder operation. The cylinder is tested with the customer's keys for rough rotation, key wear, broken wafers or pins, plug drag, loose mortise cylinder collars, and improper screw length. If rekeying is part of the repair, the plug is pinned with the proper pinning kit and checked for smooth shear-line action before it goes back in the door.

Reassembly is followed by adjustment, not just a quick turn of the key. The strike is aligned, fasteners are tightened into sound material, latch projection is verified, levers are checked for return, and any fire-rated or egress hardware is left operating without defeating required latching. The technician cycles the door repeatedly from both sides, with the closer engaged, to confirm the repair works under normal traffic conditions.

Simple Repair or Hard Diagnosis

A straightforward commercial lock repair is usually one where the failure is visible and isolated. A Schlage, Falcon, Arrow, Yale or Sargent lever with a loose through-bolt, a rim cylinder that has backed out, a mortise cylinder with a worn cam, or a storefront Adams Rite-style latch that is dry and packed with aluminum dust can often be corrected without changing the whole opening. If the key still turns cleanly, the door closes squarely, and the latch or bolt lines up with the strike, the repair is mostly careful disassembly, cleaning, lubrication, tightening, re-pinning if needed, and checking that the handle, cylinder, latch and closer are not fighting each other.

The difficult jobs are the ones where the lock is blamed for a door problem. High-traffic doors across Dauphin, Cumberland and Lebanon counties take a beating from wind, delivery carts, weather, frame movement and closers that were adjusted to slam instead of latch. A Grade 1 cylindrical lock can feel broken when the hinge side is sagging. A mortise case can chew itself up when the strike is off by an eighth of an inch. An exit device may fail because the latch is dragging, the dogging mechanism is worn, or the vertical rods are out of adjustment. In those cases, replacing the cylinder alone does not fix the real defect.

The hardest repairs are intermittent: a key that works for one employee but not another, a lever that sticks only after the sun hits the door, or a deadlatch that fails when the closer pulls the door tight. Good diagnosis means checking the key, cylinder, lock body, strike, hinges, frame and closer as one system before deciding what actually needs repair.

Before The Service Call

Before the locksmith heads to a commercial lock repair in Dauphin, Cumberland or Lebanon County, narrow the problem as much as you can. Tell us whether the key will not turn, the latch will not retract, the lever is sagging, the deadlatch is binding, the cylinder spins, or the door only fails after heavy use. A lock that works with the door open but not closed often points to alignment, hinge wear, closer pressure or a strike issue, not a bad cylinder. A lock that fails with every key may need cleaning, adjustment, repinning or cylinder repair. A lock that fails with one key may be a worn duplicate or a damaged cut.

Gather the keys that are actually used on that door, including manager keys, tenant keys, control keys for interchangeable cores, and any restricted keys for Medeco, Mul-T-Lock, Schlage Primus or other protected keyways. If you know the hardware brand, note it: Schlage, Yale, Falcon, Arrow, Corbin Russwin, SARGENT, Kwikset, Baldwin and others all service differently. Photos of the lock face, latch edge, strike plate, door label, and any broken parts help us load the van correctly.

Expect the technician to ask who is authorized to approve work on the door, especially in offices, retail spaces, warehouses, schools, medical buildings and multi-tenant properties. Clear access matters too. If the door is in constant use, plan how staff, deliveries or customers will move while the lock is tested. We may need to cycle the key, remove the cylinder, inspect the latch case, check the strike and verify egress before calling the repair complete.

Good to know

Common questions.

What is included in commercial lock repair?

Commercial lock repair usually starts with a full inspection of the door opening, then moves to the part causing the failure. That may mean repairing or re-pinning a cylinder, tightening loose hardware, replacing worn springs, adjusting the strike, or correcting latch alignment. On commercial doors, the closer, hinges, and frame condition matter just as much as the lock body.

How do you know if a commercial lock can be repaired instead of replaced?

A lock is often repairable when the body is intact, the keyway isn't badly damaged, and the problem is wear, looseness, or misalignment rather than a cracked mechanism. If the door has been forced, the case is stripped, or the hardware is obsolete and parts are unavailable, replacement may be the better answer. The inspection decides that.

What makes commercial lock repair harder than residential lock repair?

Commercial openings usually have more moving parts and more traffic. A lever lock may work with a closer, a panic device, hinges, and a specific strike setup. If one piece is off, the lock can fail even when the cylinder is fine. Higher-security hardware, mortise bodies, and frequent use all add complexity.

Can you repair high-security commercial locks like Medeco or Mul-T-Lock?

Yes, when the hardware is still serviceable. High-security cylinders often need careful keyway decoding, precise pinning, or replacement parts matched to the brand and key control system. If the issue is alignment or a worn cylinder, repair may be straightforward. If the lock was damaged by forcing or drilling, the job becomes more involved.

That matters on commercial work because the door hardware has to be assessed where it's installed. Can you repair a commercial lock without replacing the whole door hardware set?

Often, yes. A failed commercial lock may only need a latch, tailpiece, spring cage, lever return, cylinder, cam, or strike adjustment. Grade 1 and Grade 2 cylindrical locks, mortise locks, storefront deadlatches, and panic hardware all fail in different ways. If the lock body is worn past adjustment or parts are obsolete, replacement may be the better long-term repair.

Will a repaired lock still work with our existing business keys?

In many cases, the existing key system can be kept. We can repair the lock case or trim while leaving the cylinder in place, or re-pin a replacement cylinder to match your current key if the keyway allows it. Compatibility depends on the brand and keyway, such as Schlage, Kwikset, Yale, Arrow, Falcon, Medeco, Mul-T-Lock, or a restricted commercial system.

Why does our door lock work sometimes but not during busy parts of the day?

Intermittent lock trouble is common on high-traffic doors. Heat, building movement, worn hinges, closer pressure, loose mounting screws, and a misaligned strike can make a latch bind even when the lock itself is not broken. Aluminum storefront doors and hollow metal doors often need adjustment at the door, frame, strike, and closer before the lock repair is truly finished.

Can exit devices and fire-rated door hardware be repaired, or must they be replaced?

Many exit devices can be repaired with proper parts, including dogging assemblies, latch bolts, rods, trim, cylinders, and strikes. The repair has to preserve safe egress and code function. Hardware on fire-rated openings should not be modified in a way that defeats latching or listing requirements. NFPA 101 and ADA concerns matter, especially on public, medical, school, retail, and office doors.
