What commercial lock rekeying actually involves

Commercial lock rekeying starts at the hardware, not the key. In a Harrisburg storefront along 2nd or 3rd Street, or an office near the State Capitol, the cylinder inside the lock is what actually decides who can open the door. When staff changes, a key gets lost, or too many copies have circulated, we can reset that cylinder by changing the pin stack so old keys stop working and new keys do. That usually means removing the cylinder, reading the existing keyway, matching the lock brand and model, and repinning it with the right kit for the hardware in front of us. Kwikset, Schlage, Yale, Baldwin, Medeco, and Mul-T-Lock all have their own layouts and tolerances.

The process is straightforward, but the details matter. First we identify whether the lock is a standard cylinder, a mortise lock, a rim cylinder, or part of a commercial lever set with an ANSI Grade 1, 2, or 3 body. Then we test the key operation, inspect for worn pins, damaged springs, miscut keys, or a loose plug, and decide whether rekeying will solve the problem or whether the lock itself is too worn to hold a reliable key. On some jobs, we can rekey right at the door; on others, the cylinder has to be pulled and worked on at the bench. If the building uses master keying, we also have to keep the hierarchy intact so one door still responds to the right level of access while former keys are removed from the system.

What makes a job easier is having the door and old key available, plus any information on who should still have access. What makes it harder is shared entrances, damaged keys, aftermarket cylinders, or hardware that has been forced and no longer turns cleanly. In Harrisburg, where turnover can move quickly between offices, shops, and service spaces, rekeying is often the cleanest way to tighten control without changing every piece of hardware on the door.

Good to know

Common questions.

What does commercial lock rekeying actually change inside the lock?

Rekeying changes the pin combinations inside the cylinder so old keys no longer line up. The lock body stays in place; the tumbler setup is reset. On commercial hardware, that may involve standard pin stacks, spool pins, or high-security components, depending on whether the door uses Schlage, Kwikset, Medeco, or another system.

How do you know if a business lock should be rekeyed or replaced?

If the lock body is solid and the problem is limited to access control, rekeying is usually the first option. If the plug is loose, the keyway is damaged, the cam is stripped, or the mechanism binds even with the right key, replacement may be the better long-term fix. We check the cylinder, the strike, and the latch action before deciding.

Can you rekey a master key system without losing the whole setup?

Yes, if the existing hardware allows it. The goal is to change access for the affected keys while keeping the master hierarchy intact. That takes careful pinning, because each cylinder has to respond only to the correct change key and still respect the master key pattern used across the building.

What kinds of commercial locks can be rekeyed in Harrisburg buildings?

Common commercial cylinders from Schlage, Yale, Baldwin, Kwikset, Medeco, and Mul-T-Lock can often be rekeyed, as can many mortise, rim, and key-in-knob setups. Some high-security systems require specific key blanks, control cards, or manufacturer procedures, so the exact lock model matters more than the door itself.

Do I need anything ready before you come to a shop or office near the Capitol or Front Street?

Having the current key, any spare keys, and a quick list of which doors should still work helps a lot. If the building uses different access levels, note that before the technician starts. It also helps to know whether the lock is on the front entrance, a side door, or a rear service door, because each one can have different hardware.
