What commercial lock repair actually involves

When a commercial door starts hanging up, I look at the hardware before I touch the lock body. On arrival, the first checks are the door's alignment, the latch-to-strike fit, hinge wear, closers, and whether the lever or key cylinder is fighting a warped frame, loose screws, or damaged trim. In Paxtang, that matters on busy doors along Derry Street and on the tree-lined blocks near Paxtang Park, where a door that drags all day can turn a small problem into a repeated lockout.

From there, the repair depends on the failure point. A Kwikset, Schlage, Baldwin, or Yale commercial set might need re-pinning, a worn tailpiece, a tightened set screw, or a replacement cylinder; higher-security Medeco or Mul-T-Lock hardware may need careful decoding and exact keying parts. If the issue is with access control, I check the smart/proximity components, wiring at the door, and whether the lock is still latching cleanly. The goal is to fix the real cause, not just make the key turn once. Some doors need non-destructive picking to get them open for service, while others need decoding, pinning kits, or fresh hardware matched to the existing prep. The job is easier when you can tell us what the door does, which side is acting up, and whether the key still works from either side.

Commercial lock repair in a place like Paxtang usually comes down to keeping high-traffic doors usable without turning the whole entrance into a construction project. When the hardware is still sound, I repair it. When the cylinder, latch, or closer is too far gone, I explain that clearly and replace only what the door actually needs.

Common questions.

What do you check first on a commercial lock repair call in Paxtang?

I start with the door itself: hinge movement, closer function, latch alignment, and strike plate position. If the door is out of square, the lock can seem bad even when the cylinder is fine. After that, I test the keyway, latch, and any worn internal parts to find the real failure.

Can a commercial lock be repaired, or does it usually need replacement?

Many do get repaired. Loose hardware, worn cylinders, bad keys, bent latches, and shifted strikes are common fixable issues. If the body is cracked, the mechanism is badly worn, or the door prep no longer fits the hardware, replacement may make more sense than trying to force a repair.

Do you repair high-security commercial locks like Medeco or Mul-T-Lock?

Yes, but those locks need careful handling. High-security cylinders often use restricted keyways and precise pinning, so I check the exact model before working on it. Some problems are internal, while others come from the door, strike, or key wear. The repair plan depends on which part is actually failing.

What if the lock is working, but the door won't latch unless I pull it hard?

That usually points to alignment, not just the lock. The strike may be off, the hinges may be sagging, or the closer may be pushing the door out of position. I check the whole opening first, because a latch that only catches under pressure usually means the hardware is fighting the frame.
