What keyless entry systems actually involves

Keyless entry for a business sounds simple until you match the system to the door, the traffic pattern, and the level of control the owner actually needs. In the Bridge Street business district in New Cumberland, that usually means choosing between a keypad lock, a fob-based setup, or a fuller access-control system. A straightforward job is a single-door keypad on a standard commercial lever set. More complicated work starts when you need audit trails, multiple users, restricted schedules, or a door that already has worn hardware, bad alignment, or an older cylinder that interferes with the new electronics.

The process starts with the door itself: latch, strike, closer, hinge wear, and whether the existing hardware is ANSI Grade 1, 2, or 3. After that, the tech checks power source, wiring path if there is one, and whether the lock body can accept the new function without weakening the door. For fob and access-control systems, programming matters just as much as installation. That can include enrolling users, setting master credentials, testing a reader, and confirming that lost credentials can be removed cleanly. Some jobs are easier when the customer knows how many users need access and which doors should stay restricted. Others take more care if the building sits near the riverfront blocks, where moisture and heavy use can affect keypads, contacts, and hardware over time.

Commercial brands like Schlage, Kwikset, Yale, Baldwin, Medeco, and Mul-T-Lock all come into play depending on the door and security target. The right setup is the one that works with the building, not against it.

Good to know

Common questions.

What is the difference between a keypad lock, a fob system, and access control for a business?

A keypad lock lets approved users enter a code. A fob system uses a credential that the reader recognizes, which is handy when you want to add or remove users without changing a code. Access control goes further, letting you manage multiple doors, user permissions, and sometimes entry logs. The best choice depends on how many people need access and how tightly you want to control it.

What makes a commercial keyless entry job easy or difficult?

It is easier when the door is standard, the latch is in good shape, and the customer knows how many users need access. It gets harder when the door is warped, the closer is misadjusted, the hardware is worn, or the building needs multiple schedules and restricted entry points. Electronics work best when the mechanical side of the door is already right.

Can a keyless entry system be installed on an existing commercial door in New Cumberland?

Often, yes. Many keypad and fob systems can be fit to an existing commercial door if the hardware, bore, and door condition are suitable. The technician checks the lock body, strike, hinge condition, and whether the door can support the new hardware without weakening it. If the lock is already worn or mismatched, some repair work may come first.

What should I have ready before calling about keyless entry for my Bridge Street business?

Have the door location, how many people need access, and whether you want one door or several controlled. It also helps to know if the door currently uses a deadbolt, lever lock, panic hardware, or an older card or keypad system. If you can describe the problem clearly, the technician can match the right hardware and programming to the site faster.

Can a locksmith program fobs or keyless credentials for commercial use?

Yes. Depending on the system, the tech can enroll fobs, set user permissions, and remove lost or outdated credentials. Some systems use standalone programming at the lock, while others use a controller or software tied to the building. The exact process depends on the brand and whether the door is a simple keypad setup or a fuller access-control installation.
