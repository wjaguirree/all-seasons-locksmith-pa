What commercial lock installation actually involves

Commercial lock installation starts with the door, not the lock. On storefronts and office entries in Hershey, I look at the slab, frame, hinge side, latch style, strike location, and existing prep before a new lock ever comes out of the truck. That matters because a heavy-duty commercial lock has to line up cleanly or it will bind, scrape, or wear out the door. For many jobs, the goal is a clean install on wood, metal, or aluminum storefront hardware with as little alteration as possible, especially on doors along Chocolate Avenue where daily traffic never really slows down.

After that first check, the work usually comes down to measuring backset, confirming handing, choosing the right cylinder and keyway, and matching the hardware grade to the door's use. A typical install may involve drilling or reusing existing prep, setting a new deadbolt, cylindrical lock, or mortise-style commercial set, then aligning the strike so the latch throws smoothly. Brands like Schlage, Kwikset, Baldwin, Yale, Medeco, and Mul-T-Lock each bring different cylinder and security options, and commercial entries often need ANSI Grade 1 or Grade 2 hardware rather than basic residential parts. If the door has an odd cutout, damaged edge, or old lock holes that do not match the new set, the job gets more involved. Having the door type, current hardware, and whether you want master keying or restricted key control figured out ahead of time helps the process move faster and cleaner.

Good to know

Common questions.

How is a commercial lock installation done without damaging the door?

The process starts with measuring the existing prep and checking the door, frame, and strike before any drilling happens. A careful install uses the smallest amount of new cutting needed, keeps the latch and strike lined up, and avoids forcing hardware into a misfit opening. That reduces finish damage and helps the door close smoothly.

What kind of commercial locks work best for Hershey storefronts and offices?

That depends on the door and the traffic it sees. Many storefronts and offices use ANSI Grade 1 or Grade 2 cylindrical or mortise hardware, while higher-security doors may use Medeco or Mul-T-Lock cylinders. Schlage, Yale, Baldwin, and Kwikset also have commercial-grade options, but the right choice depends on the existing prep and how the door is used.

Can you install a new lock if the door already has old holes or a different lock style?

Often, yes. Old prep can sometimes be reused, but the new lock has to match the bore, backset, handing, and strike placement. If the old holes are off or the previous hardware was a different style, the door may need repair or re-drilling. A good fit matters more than forcing a lock into place.

What should I have ready before scheduling commercial lock installation in Hershey?

It helps to know the door material, whether it's a storefront, office, or side entry, and what hardware is on the door now. If you want the new lock keyed alike, master keyed, or matched to an existing key system, have that information ready too. Clear access to the door and the old hardware speeds the job.

Do you install locks for businesses near Chocolate Avenue and Hersheypark Drive?

Yes. Shops, restaurants, and offices along Chocolate Avenue, near Hersheypark Drive, and around Downtown Hershey often need hardware that can handle heavy daily use. The local mix of visitors and shift workers means doors see a lot of traffic, so the install needs to be aligned correctly and built for repeated use, not just a quick swap.
