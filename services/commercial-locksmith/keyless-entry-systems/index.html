What keyless entry systems actually involves

Keyless entry systems still depend on real hardware: a keypad, reader, strike, latch, power supply, wiring, and the lock body on the door. When one piece starts failing, the whole entry point can act strange. A keypad may stop reading numbers cleanly, a fob reader may blink but not release the latch, or an electric strike may click without opening because the door and frame are out of alignment. On commercial doors, we also look at the exit device, hinges, closer, and whether the existing lockset is built for ANSI Grade 1 or Grade 2 use.

The first step is figuring out what the system is actually doing, not just what it looks like from the outside. We test power, reader response, relay output, and the mechanical side of the door. If the issue is a lost fob or a code that needs to be removed, we work through the controller or standalone keypad logic. If the problem is more physical, we may repair wiring, replace a worn actuator, rekey or repin cylinders on the associated hardware, or swap in compatible Schlage, Kwikset, Yale, Baldwin, Medeco, or Mul-T-Lock components where the application calls for it. For higher-security jobs, we may also use card-based access, smart/proximity credentials, or keypads tied to a fuller access-control setup.

What makes these jobs easier is clear access to the door, known equipment models, and any existing master key or programming information the business already has on hand. What makes them harder is water damage, damaged wiring inside the frame, mixed hardware from different manufacturers, or doors that have been forced and no longer line up correctly. A good repair solves both sides of the opening: the electronics that authorize entry and the physical lock parts that have to move every time the door is used. Keyless Entry System Types

Commercial keyless entry is not one product. The simplest version is a standalone keypad lock, often used on a storage room, office suite door or employee entrance where only a handful of codes are needed. Some replace the existing cylindrical or mortise lock; others are narrow-stile units for aluminum storefront doors. Brands such as Schlage, Yale, Alarm Lock and Trilogy make models that can be programmed at the door, which keeps the setup straightforward but also means code changes must be handled locally.

Fob and card systems work differently. Instead of memorized codes, users carry a credential: proximity fob, HID-style card, smart card or mobile credential depending on the reader and controller. These systems usually separate the reader, electric strike or maglock, door position switch and control panel. That matters in practice because a failed reader is not the same problem as a failed lock, and a door that does not latch cleanly can make a good access system act unreliable. Multi-door systems may also include audit trails, schedules, user groups and remote credential changes.

There are also hybrid jobs. A business may keep a mechanical key override, add a keypad to one door, use fobs on another, and tie a main entrance into an existing alarm or intercom. Exterior doors in Dauphin, Cumberland and Lebanon counties have to deal with weather, door sag, salt, power interruptions and daily abuse. The right variant depends less on the brochure and more on the door, frame, traffic level, required egress, and who will manage the users after installation.

Hardware Behind Keyless Entry

Commercial keyless entry is not one device. On doors across Dauphin, Cumberland and Lebanon counties, the work may touch standalone keypad locks, proximity readers, electric strikes, maglocks, electrified lever sets, exit devices, door contacts, request-to-exit buttons, power supplies and the mechanical lock already in the door. Common hardware includes Alarm Lock Trilogy keypads, Schlage CO and AD series locks, Yale and SARGENT institutional levers, HES electric strikes, Von Duprin electrified panic hardware, Adams Rite storefront locks, RCI and Securitron magnetic locks, plus LCN or Norton closers that have to control the door properly after the access hardware is installed.

The door type matters as much as the electronics. A hollow metal office door with a cylindrical Grade 1 lever is a different job than an aluminum storefront door with an Adams Rite deadlatch, or a fire-rated corridor door with a rim exit device. Electric strikes must match the latch, frame depth, voltage and fail-secure or fail-safe requirement. Maglocks need correct release hardware and code-compliant egress; they cannot simply be bolted above a door and called finished. Panic hardware has to respect NFPA 101 life-safety rules, and many public-facing doors must keep ADA-compliant operation.

A good system also leaves the mechanical side serviceable. Cylinders, cores, restricted keyways, latch alignment, hinges and closers still matter, because a keypad cannot fix a dragging door or a worn latch. The best installations make the electronics and the lock hardware work as one system, not as two separate problems fighting each other.

Installation Sequence and Checks

A keyless entry job starts at the door, not at the keypad. The technician first identifies the opening: hollow metal, aluminum storefront, wood, glass/aluminum, or fire-rated assembly. Then the existing hardware is checked in order — closer, hinges, latch alignment, strike prep, exit device, cylinder, lever trim, and frame condition. A door that drags, rebounds, or fails to latch will not become reliable just because a keypad or reader is added. The tech also determines whether the opening needs fail-secure electric strike, fail-safe maglock, electrified lever, standalone keypad lock, or a small access-control panel with reader and power supply.

After the hardware choice is confirmed, the technician lays out cable paths, mounting height, reader location, and power location. On access-control systems, low-voltage wiring is run for the reader, lock, request-to-exit device, door position switch, and power supply or controller. Polarity, voltage drop, relay behavior, and lock current draw are checked before final connections are made. For exit doors, the tech verifies that egress is not blocked and that the setup stays consistent with ADA clearance requirements and NFPA 101 life-safety rules for exit hardware.

Final setup is more than making the door buzz open. The technician enrolls PINs, fobs, cards, or mobile credentials, sets schedules if the hardware supports them, labels circuits, tests mechanical key override where present, and checks both normal power and power-loss behavior. The door is opened and closed repeatedly from both sides. The last checks are simple but important: latch capture, reader response, audit trail if available, credential removal, and whether staff can operate it without guessing.

Why Some Installs Get Complicated

A straightforward keyless entry job is usually a single door with sound hardware, a clean frame, known credentials, and a clear decision on who gets access. A common example is an office suite or employee entrance where the existing cylindrical lock, mortise lock, or exit device is in good shape and the door closes squarely every time. If the latch lines up, the closer is adjusted, the strike is not chewed up, and there is a usable power source or a good battery-operated keypad option, the work is mostly about choosing the right device, setting the handing, programming codes or fobs, and testing entry and egress.

The hard jobs are rarely hard because of the keypad itself. They get difficult because the door is already fighting the lock. Aluminum storefront doors with worn pivots, hollow metal doors with twisted frames, rim exit devices with poor dogging, or fire-rated openings that cannot be modified casually all change the plan. Adding a reader to a door with an electrified strike, maglock, REX motion sensor, door position switch, or tie-in to an alarm panel means the wiring, power supply, fail-safe or fail-secure behavior, and code-compliant egress all have to be right. NFPA 101 and ADA rules matter on commercial openings, especially where panic hardware is involved.

Customers can make the job cleaner by knowing who manages the building, whether the door is fire-rated, how many users need credentials, and whether lost fobs or former employees are part of the problem. Photos of both sides of the door, the frame, the existing lock, and any closer or exit device help separate a simple keypad conversion from a real access-control project before holes are drilled.

Prepare Before the Visit

Before a technician comes out for a keyless entry system, decide what you actually need the door to do. Count the openings involved: front customer door, employee entrance, office suite door, stockroom, server room, pharmacy cage, mechanical room, or shared tenant entrance. For each one, decide who should get in, when they should get in, and whether you need an audit trail showing which PIN, fob, card, or mobile credential was used. A simple standalone keypad is very different from a networked access-control panel with schedules, groups, lockout dates, and reporting. Also think through turnover. If employees, cleaners, vendors, or subcontractors change often, fobs or managed credentials usually beat shared codes.

Gather whatever you already have on the door and the building. Photos help: the full door, the edge of the door, the frame, the existing lock, any exit device, closer, electric strike, maglock, reader, power supply, or door contact. Note brands if visible, such as Schlage, Yale, Alarm Lock, Sargent, Von Duprin, Adams Rite, HES, or Securitron. Bring existing mechanical keys, admin codes, programming cards, fobs, and any access-control software login you are authorized to use. If the door is fire-rated, has panic hardware, serves an assembly space, or must meet ADA egress requirements, say so before hardware is chosen.

Expect the locksmith to inspect the door before recommending equipment. A sagging aluminum storefront door, hollow metal frame, warped wood door, weak closer, or misaligned latch can make good electronics act unreliable. The visit usually includes checking door swing, latch engagement, power options, cable path, credential type, and whether fail-safe or fail-secure locking is appropriate for that opening.

Good to know

Common questions.

How do keyless entry systems fail on commercial doors?

The failure is usually mechanical, electrical, or both. Common problems include worn keypads, dead readers, weak power supplies, damaged wiring in the door or frame, and strikes that no longer line up with the latch. Even a good access system can seem faulty if the closer, hinges, or exit device are out of adjustment.

Can you repair just the keypad, fob reader, or strike without replacing the whole system?

Often, yes. A lot depends on the brand, the model, and whether the rest of the setup is still compatible. If the controller is fine, we may replace a failed reader, service the electric strike, or correct wiring and programming instead of changing every component. Matching parts matters, especially on commercial doors.

What should I have ready before calling for keyless entry service?

Have the door location, the type of system you use, and any brand names you can see on the keypad, reader, strike, or controller. If you have old fobs, admin codes, or an existing access list, that helps too. Knowing whether the door is a storefront, office entrance, or interior controlled opening also speeds diagnosis.

Can a business switch from keys to keyless entry on an existing door?

Usually, yes, if the door and frame can support the hardware. We look at the lockset, power route, latch type, and whether the opening needs a keypad, fob reader, or full access-control setup. Some doors only need a new lock body and strike; others need wiring and a control unit added.

Do keyless systems still use mechanical lock hardware?

Absolutely. The electronics authorize entry, but the door still needs a latch, strike, cylinder, and a solid frame to work correctly. On many commercial openings, the best setup is a hybrid one: electronic access on the outside with a properly fitted mechanical lock or exit device on the inside. That way the door stays usable if one side fails.

Can keyless entry be added to existing commercial doors?

Often, yes, but the door hardware matters. A cylindrical lever, mortise lock, rim exit device, concealed vertical rod exit device, and aluminum storefront lock all call for different equipment. We check door swing, frame depth, latch condition, closer adjustment, and available wiring path before recommending keypad locks, electrified strikes, maglocks, or access-control readers for businesses in Dauphin, Cumberland, and Lebanon counties.

What happens if power or internet goes out?

A properly designed system should fail the right way for the opening. Some doors need battery backup, some locks remain secure when power drops, and life-safety doors must still allow egress. Cloud-managed systems may lose remote administration during an internet outage, but local credentials can often continue working. This depends on the controller, lock type, and how the system was programmed.

Can one fob work across multiple doors or locations?

Yes, if the system is planned that way. Multi-door and multi-site access control can assign one credential to a front entrance, employee door, stockroom, office suite, or restricted area, with different schedules for each. The details vary by platform, reader format, controller capacity, and whether the building already has legacy fobs that must be matched, migrated, or replaced.

Do keyless systems meet fire and egress requirements?

They can, but hardware choice is critical. Doors used for emergency exit must release correctly under NFPA 101 life-safety rules and cannot trap occupants behind a keypad, reader, or magnetic lock. Exit devices, request-to-exit sensors, door position switches, and ADA-compliant operation may all be part of the design. We verify the opening before installing access control on commercial egress doors.
