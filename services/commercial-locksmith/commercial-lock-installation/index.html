What commercial lock installation actually involves

Commercial lock installation is not the same job as swapping a residential knob on a front door. A storefront, office, or back-of-house entrance needs hardware that can handle daily traffic, resist prying, and fit the door, frame, and closing setup as a system. Dealerships and big-box stores can hand over a box; a commercial locksmith has to check the door thickness, handing, latch alignment, strike location, and whether the opening should take a cylindrical lock, mortise lock, exit device trim, or a deadbolt built for commercial use. For many jobs, that means brands like Schlage, Kwikset commercial lines, Baldwin, Yale, Medeco, or Mul-T-Lock, with the right grade of hardware for the opening.

The process usually starts with a walk-through of the door and frame. The technician measures the bore and edge prep, checks whether the door is hollow metal, aluminum storefront, wood, or steel, and looks for previous patch work, worn hinges, or a failing closer that can throw the latch out of line. If the lock needs to be keyed to an existing system, the cylinder can often be pinned on site with a pinning kit; if the business wants tighter control, restricted or high-security keyways may be discussed. When a door needs new keys from scratch, we can originate them by code, decode an existing cylinder, or work from the hardware on hand. The harder jobs are usually the ones with misaligned frames, damaged prep, or mixed hardware from past repairs. Having the old lock, key, and any building access instructions ready helps move the work along cleanly.

Good commercial installation is about making the lock work with the door instead of fighting it. That means testing the latch throw, strike engagement, key function, and closing action before the job is done, then making the small adjustments that keep the door from binding later.

Commercial Lock Types Installed

Commercial lock installation is not one single job. A glass storefront door usually takes a narrow-stile aluminum deadlatch or hookbolt, often Adams Rite style hardware, fitted into an aluminum frame with a mortise cylinder or interchangeable core. The work is exacting because the lock body, faceplate, strike, paddle or pull, and door closer all have to agree with each other. If the door has sagged or the threshold is worn, a new lock can still bind unless the opening is corrected during the install.

Office suite doors are different. Hollow metal and solid wood doors often use cylindrical lever locks from Schlage, Falcon, Arrow, Yale, or similar commercial lines, commonly in ANSI/BHMA Grade 1 or Grade 2. These are bored through the door and must be set for the correct backset, latch throw, handedness, and keyway. A manager's office, records room, or server closet may call for a heavier mortise lock or a deadbolt added above the lever, but that depends on door thickness, frame strength, and whether the door must allow code-compliant egress.

There are also keyed-entry, storeroom, classroom, privacy, passage, and communicating functions, and they behave very differently in daily use. A storeroom lock stays locked from the outside and is common for stockrooms. A keyed-entry function can be left unlocked, which is convenient but easier to misuse. Exit doors may require panic hardware or an exit device rather than a standard knob or lever, especially where NFPA 101 and ADA egress rules matter. The right installation starts with how the door is actually used, not just what fits in the hole.

Commercial Lock Hardware We Install

Commercial lock installation is not one kind of lock. On storefront aluminum doors, the work often involves Adams Rite-style narrow-stile mortise locks, hookbolts, deadlatches, paddle handles, and cylinders built for aluminum frames. On office suites and interior commercial doors, it is more often ANSI/BHMA Grade 1 or Grade 2 cylindrical levers from brands such as Schlage, Arrow, Falcon, Yale, or Best, with the correct backset, latch throw, strike, and lever function for that opening. A private office may need an office-function lever; a storage room may need storeroom function so it stays locked from the outside; a restroom or utility room may call for different hardware entirely.

We also install commercial deadbolts, mortise cylinders, rim cylinders, interchangeable core hardware, and high-security restricted keyway cylinders where key control matters. That can include Medeco, Mul-T-Lock, Schlage Primus-style systems, SFIC cores, LFIC cores, and conventional pin-tumbler cylinders depending on the door, the keying plan, and who needs access. The actual installation touches more than the lock body: latch prep, through-bolt holes, strike alignment, door closer behavior, frame condition, reveal, and whether the door is wood, hollow metal, or aluminum storefront. A strong Grade 1 lock performs poorly if the strike is floating in a damaged frame or the latch barely engages.

Some doors also require electrified hardware or preparation for access control, but the lock still has to function mechanically. Fail-secure electric strikes, request-to-exit trim, and keyed override cylinders all depend on the same fundamentals: correct handing, clean prep, square mounting, and hardware that matches the door's use.

Installation From Survey To Test

A commercial lock installation starts at the door, not at the catalog. The technician checks the door material, thickness, handing, swing, frame condition, existing prep, backset, latch edge, strike location, and whether the opening is a wood door, hollow metal door, aluminum storefront door, or glass-and-aluminum entrance. On office suites and storefronts in Dauphin, Cumberland, and Lebanon counties, that survey decides whether the correct hardware is a Grade 1 or Grade 2 cylindrical lever, mortise lock, deadlatch, deadbolt, interconnected lock, or trim that must work with an exit device. We also check egress requirements before drilling, because a lock that secures well but blocks legal exit is the wrong lock.

Next, the technician lays out the prep using the manufacturer template, verifies centerlines, and protects the door face before cutting. Existing holes may be reused if they are clean and in the right position; wallowed-out bores, misaligned latch pockets, or damaged aluminum stiles may need reinforcement plates, conversion plates, or a different lock body. The lock is then fitted, through-bolts are set where required, the latch or deadlatch is aligned, and the strike is cut or adjusted so the latch fully throws without dragging. On keyed hardware, the cylinder is installed and checked for proper tailpiece length, cam direction, and smooth key operation.

Before leaving, the technician tests the door open and closed, from both sides, with the closer operating normally. We check lever return, latch engagement, deadlatch function, key control, ADA-friendly operation where applicable, and whether weatherstrip or frame movement is affecting the lock. A good installation should not need the user to lift, shove, or pull the door to make the key turn.

Simple Fits Versus Hard Installs

A straightforward commercial lock installation is usually a clean door with the right prep already in it. If a hollow metal office door is already bored for a cylindrical lever, the backset matches the new lock, the latch edge is square, and the frame strike is solid, the work is mostly careful fitting: verify handing, set the latch, tighten through-bolts, align the strike, and test the key and lever under real closing pressure. The same is true on many storefront aluminum doors when the existing Adams Rite-style prep matches the new deadlatch or hookbolt hardware and the door is not sagging in the opening.

The hard jobs start when the door, frame, and lock are not speaking the same language. We see this across commercial buildings in Dauphin, Cumberland, and Lebanon counties: older wood office doors with blown-out bore holes, hollow metal frames patched three times, glass storefront doors with worn pivots, and exterior doors where weatherstripping pushes the latch out of alignment. Installing a Schlage, Yale, Arrow, Falcon, Baldwin, Medeco, or Mul-T-Lock product is not just swapping parts if the stile is narrow, the backset is unusual, the door is fire-rated, or the existing prep was drilled off-center. Sometimes the correct answer is a wrap plate, filler plate, latch guard, different function lock, or frame repair before the new lock goes on.

The difficult part is not making the lock fit once. It is making it keep working after employees use it all day. A Grade 1 lever can still fail early if the strike is misaligned or the door closer slams the latch into the frame. Good installation means checking the whole opening before drilling metal that cannot be undrilled.

Before The Installer Arrives

Before a commercial lock installation, decide who needs access and how the door is supposed to behave after business hours. A front customer entrance may need a Grade 1 or Grade 2 lever lock, deadlatch, mortise lock, or storefront hook bolt depending on the door. An office suite may only need a passage lever with a keyed deadbolt, while a stockroom, records room, server closet, or employee-only area may need storeroom function so it stays locked from the outside. If you use Schlage, Kwikset, Yale, Arrow, Falcon, Baldwin, Medeco, or Mul-T-Lock elsewhere in the building, tell the technician so the new hardware can be planned around the existing key system instead of creating unnecessary extra keys.

Gather any working keys, master keys, restricted keyway authorization cards, door schedules, prior hardware packaging, and building-management requirements you have. If the property is leased, confirm whether the landlord requires a certain finish, keyway, fire-rating label, or brand. On aluminum storefront doors, note whether the lock is a narrow-stile deadlatch, Adams Rite-style lock body, paddle, thumbturn, or keyed cylinder. On wood or hollow-metal office doors, check whether the door already has a cylindrical bore, mortise prep, deadbolt prep, or an old wrap plate covering damage.

Expect the technician to look at the door, frame, latch alignment, closer action, hinges, strike prep, ADA usability, and egress requirements before drilling anything. A strong lock on a sagging door is still a bad installation. Clearing merchandise, desks, file cabinets, mats, or display racks from the work area helps the job go cleaner and usually lets the installation be completed in one visit.

Good to know

Common questions.

What kind of lock is best for a commercial door?

It depends on the door and how the entry is used. A storefront, office, or storage door may need a cylindrical lock, mortise lock, deadbolt, or exit device trim. The right choice also depends on door material, traffic, and whether the opening needs keyed access, panic hardware, or higher-security key control. Not every commercial door should use the same hardware.

Can you install a commercial lock on an existing door without replacing the whole door?

Usually, yes. Many commercial locks can be installed on the existing opening if the door and frame are still in good shape. The tech checks the bore, backset, edge prep, strike, and hinge condition first. If the door is warped, damaged, or missing proper prep, some repair work may be needed before the new lock will function correctly.

What makes commercial lock installation harder than residential lock work?

Commercial doors often have stricter hardware requirements and less room for error. Hollow metal, aluminum storefront, and fire-rated openings can all change the hardware choice. A small alignment problem can stop the latch from engaging. The tech may also need to match the lock to existing keyways, door closers, or access control parts, which adds another layer of setup.

Can you key a new commercial lock to our existing keys?

Often, yes. If the cylinder and keyway support it, the lock can be pinned to match an existing key system with the right pinning kit and keying information. The technician will need the current key or cylinder information to verify compatibility. In some cases, a restricted or high-security system like Medeco or Mul-T-Lock may be a better long-term fit.

Do you handle commercial lock installation across Dauphin, Cumberland, and Lebanon counties?

Yes. This service is region-wide across Central Pennsylvania, including Dauphin, Cumberland, and Lebanon counties. Because the work is fully mobile, the technician comes to the door, checks the opening on site, and installs the correct hardware there. Having the door location, lock brand if known, and any current keys ready helps the visit go smoothly.

Can a commercial lock be installed on an existing storefront or office door?

Usually, yes, but the door prep matters. Wood, hollow metal and aluminum storefront doors use different lock bodies, backsets, latch styles and strike plates. A Schlage or Falcon cylindrical lever will not fit the same cutout as a mortise lock, and narrow-stile aluminum doors often need Adams Rite-style hardware. We check the door thickness, frame condition, handing, existing holes and latch alignment before recommending hardware.

When is ANSI/BHMA Grade 1 necessary instead of Grade 2?

Grade 1 hardware is built for heavier use and more abuse, so it is the better choice for main entrances, employee doors, shared office corridors, service doors and tenant spaces with steady traffic. Grade 2 can be appropriate for private offices, storage rooms or lower-use interior doors. The door and frame still have to support the upgrade; a strong lock on a weak frame is not a complete installation.

Can new commercial locks be installed to work with an existing master key system?

In many cases, yes. If the existing system uses a serviceable keyway such as Schlage, Yale, Arrow, Falcon or another standard commercial cylinder, new cylinders can often be pinned to the current master key chart. Restricted or high-security systems such as Medeco or Mul-T-Lock may require authorization and matching components. If the old keying records are missing, the system may need decoding or a planned rekey.

What changes if the door has an electric strike, card reader or exit device?

Access control doors need more coordination than a standard lever installation. The mechanical lock, electric strike or latch retraction hardware, door closer, power transfer and reader schedule all have to work together. Exit devices also have code requirements, including proper egress under NFPA 101 and ADA-related operability concerns. In Dauphin, Cumberland and Lebanon counties, we commonly see older doors where wiring and hardware were added in stages, so compatibility must be verified on site.
