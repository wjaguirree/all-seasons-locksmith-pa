What panic bar installation actually involves

Panic bar installation looks simple from the hallway, but the work behind it depends heavily on what kind of door you have. On newer commercial openings, we usually start by checking the door material, frame condition, handing, and existing hardware prep so the exit device matches the opening cleanly. On older properties across Dauphin, Cumberland, and Lebanon counties, the challenge is often different: warped wood doors, patched metal, loose hinges, or frames that have been altered over the years. That means measuring carefully, verifying latch engagement, and sometimes adapting the installation so the bar releases easily without binding.

The process is methodical. We identify whether the opening needs a rim device, surface vertical rod, concealed vertical rod, or a mortise-style exit device, then confirm the door's fire rating and hardware compatibility before any drilling or cutting starts. For many jobs, that includes checking the closer, strike alignment, and whether the existing lockset or trim must be removed. Hardware brands such as Von Duprin, Detex, Sargent, Schlage, and Falcon are common in commercial work, and the best choice depends on the door, traffic level, and code requirements. If the opening is in rough shape, the job can take extra time because the door may need reinforcement, new prep holes, or hinge repairs to let the device function the way it should.

Good panic hardware has to do two things at once: let people out fast and keep the door secured when it is closed. That balance is why a proper install matters more than the handle on the box. If you are planning an upgrade or replacing a failed device, have the door type, frame material, and any existing hardware details ready when you call (223) 240-3505.

Panic Bar Device Types

Panic bar installation is not one-size-fits-all. The simplest version is a rim exit device, where the latch sits on the surface of the door and catches a strike on the frame. Rim devices are common on single metal or aluminum storefront doors because they are durable, serviceable, and usually require less door modification than other styles. Von Duprin, Adams Rite, Yale, Sargent, Falcon, Arrow, and Detex all make devices in this category, but the right model depends on door material, stile width, handing, latch projection, and whether outside trim is needed.

Double doors change the job. If there is no center mullion, a vertical rod exit device may be required, with top and bottom latching into the header and threshold or floor. Surface vertical rods mount on the face of the door; concealed vertical rods run inside the door and require more precise prep. Concealed rods look cleaner and are harder to tamper with, but they are less forgiving on older doors that sag, twist, or have worn pivots. A removable mullion can allow two rim devices instead, which often makes service and future hardware changes simpler.

Fire-rated openings are their own category. A fire exit device must be listed for that use and cannot be dogged open unless it uses listed electric dogging tied to the proper system. Electrified panic hardware adds another layer: electric latch retraction, delayed egress, alarmed exit devices, and access-control trim all require careful coordination so the door still releases correctly for egress under NFPA 101 and ADA requirements. Across Dauphin, Cumberland, and Lebanon counties, the practical difference is usually not the bar itself; it is matching the device to the door, frame, occupancy, and inspection requirement.

Exit Device Hardware We Handle

Panic bar installation is not one piece of hardware slapped on a door. The device has to match the door, frame, latch point, traffic use, and code purpose. We work with rim exit devices on single doors, mortise exit devices where the lock case sits inside the door edge, and surface or concealed vertical rod devices for pairs of doors where the top and bottom latches secure into the header and threshold. Common commercial lines include Von Duprin, Detex, Sargent, Yale, Falcon, Arrow, Adams Rite, and similar ANSI/BHMA-rated hardware. On aluminum storefront doors, the work often involves narrow-stile devices and proper strikes; on hollow metal or solid wood doors, it may involve sex bolts, reinforced mounting, and closer coordination with existing lock preps.

The mechanism matters. A rim device uses a surface latch and strike. A mortise exit device uses an internal case, trim spindle, and pocket strike alignment that leaves less room for error. Vertical rod devices add latch timing, rod length, dogging position, and top/bottom bolt engagement to the job. Fire-rated openings also change the hardware choice: mechanical dogging is not allowed on many fire exit devices, and the label on the door and frame cannot be ignored. Electrified options add another layer, such as electric latch retraction, request-to-exit switches, alarmed exit devices, delayed egress where permitted, and exterior lever trim tied to access control. The wrong device can bind, fail to latch, violate egress rules, or damage the door long before the hardware is worn out.

Panic Bar Installation Steps

The job starts with the door, not the box the hardware came in. On site in Dauphin, Cumberland, or Lebanon County, the technician first confirms the door swing, door material, width, stile size, frame condition, and whether the opening is single, paired, rim, mortise, concealed vertical rod, or surface vertical rod. Existing holes are measured before anything is drilled. The exit path, latch side clearances, threshold, weatherstrip, astragal, closer, coordinator, and any electric strike or alarm contact are checked because a panic bar that latches poorly is not a compliant installation. The device type is matched to the opening, with attention to ANSI/BHMA grade, fire-rating labels, ADA operating height and force, and NFPA 101 egress requirements where they apply.

After layout, the technician marks the centerline, trims the device if the manufacturer allows it, drills or through-bolts the chassis, and installs the latch, strike, end cap, cover, and outside trim if the door uses a keyed lever, pull, or night latch. For fire-rated doors, fasteners and strikes must match the listing; field modifications are kept within what the door and hardware manufacturer permits. On pairs of doors, rod length, top and bottom latch engagement, and coordinator sequence matter. On storefront aluminum, the technician watches for narrow-stile limits and reinforced mounting points so the device does not flex loose.

Before leaving, the bar is cycled repeatedly with the door open and closed. The checks include full latch retraction, positive relatching, no binding under door-closer pressure, proper strike alignment, dogging function if allowed, outside trim operation, and clear egress without keys, tight grasping, or special knowledge. Any adjustment is made then, because the last test is the one a person depends on during an exit.

Easy Doors, Hard Installs

A straightforward panic bar installation starts with a commercial door and frame that were built for exit hardware in the first place. A solid hollow-metal door, square in the opening, with a sound closer, good hinges, and enough stile width will usually accept a rim exit device cleanly. If the door is already prepped for a common footprint, the work is mostly layout, through-bolting, strike alignment, dogging or fire-rated setup, and testing the latch so it releases with light pressure across the bar. Aluminum storefront doors can also be clean installs when the rails are wide enough and the frame has the right strike surface.

The hard jobs are the ones where the door tells a different story than the building use requires. Narrow-stile glass doors, pairs of doors with removable mullions, warped hollow-metal doors, rusted frames, sagging hinges, or an out-of-square opening can turn a simple device into a fit-and-function problem. Vertical rod devices need careful top and bottom latch alignment; concealed rods are even less forgiving. Mortise exit devices require accurate pocket work. Fire-rated doors cannot be cut, drilled, or modified casually without respecting the label. If an alarm kit, outside trim, electric latch retraction, or access control is involved, wire routing and power transfer matter too.

The honest answer is that the bar itself is only part of the job. The door has to close, latch, and release correctly every time while still meeting egress requirements such as ADA operability and NFPA 101 exit function. Sometimes repair or adjustment must come before installation, because a new panic device will not fix a twisted door or a failing frame.

Before The Van Rolls

Before scheduling panic bar installation, decide what the door has to do besides let people out. A rear employee door may only need exit-only operation. A public entrance may need outside pull trim, a keyed cylinder, lever trim, electric latch retraction, delayed-egress hardware where allowed, or connection to an access control system. If the door is fire-rated, say so up front; fire exit hardware, latch projection, and door modifications are not optional details. If the opening has double doors, note whether there is a removable mullion, an active/inactive leaf, surface vertical rods, concealed rods, or an astragal. Those details determine whether a rim device, mortise device, or vertical-rod exit device is appropriate.

Gather clear photos of both sides of the door, the frame, hinges, closer, threshold, existing lock, and any labels on the door edge or frame. Useful measurements include door width, height, thickness, stile width on aluminum storefront doors, and whether the door is hollow metal, wood, fiberglass, or narrow-stile aluminum. Have any building paperwork available if you have it: occupancy type, fire marshal notes, inspection correction notices, access control drawings, or previous hardware schedules. In Dauphin, Cumberland, and Lebanon counties, requirements can vary by use and inspection authority, so the technician will still verify the opening on site.

Expect the locksmith to check swing direction, frame condition, latch alignment, closer control, ADA operating height, and whether the door can be prepared without weakening it. A panic bar is not a cover-up for a sagging hinge, twisted frame, rotted wood, or dragging threshold. If those problems are present, they must be corrected for the exit device to latch, release, and pass inspection reliably.

Good to know

Common questions.

What is the difference between a panic bar and an exit device?

In commercial locksmith work, the terms often overlap. A panic bar is the push bar people use to open the door quickly from the inside. An exit device is the complete assembly, including the latch mechanism, trim, rods, and related hardware. The right choice depends on the door, code requirements, and whether the opening needs rim, mortise, or vertical-rod hardware.

What has to be checked before installing panic hardware on an older door?

Older doors usually need a close look at the frame, hinges, closer, and edge condition before anything is drilled. If the door is warped, patched, or not closing squarely, the device can bind or fail to latch. We also verify handing, door thickness, fire rating, and existing prep so the hardware fits the opening correctly.

Can you install panic bars on fire-rated doors in Central Pennsylvania?

Yes, but the hardware has to match the door's listing and the way the opening is used. Fire doors need approved exit devices, proper latching, and compatible trim. The installer should confirm the rating, closers, and any required labeling before fitting the device. That is especially important in commercial buildings across Dauphin, Cumberland, and Lebanon counties.

What types of panic hardware are most common?

The most common styles are rim exit devices, surface vertical rod devices, concealed vertical rod devices, and mortise exit devices. Rim devices are common on many single doors. Vertical rod setups are often used on taller or heavier doors. The correct style depends on the door construction, traffic, and whether the opening already has factory prep.

What should I have ready when I call for panic bar installation?

If you can, have the door location, door material, whether it is fire-rated, and any visible brand names or photos of the existing hardware. That helps us identify the opening faster and bring the right parts. If the door has access control or unusual trim, mention that too so the install can be planned around it.

Can a panic bar be installed on an existing commercial door?

Often, yes, but the door and frame have to be checked first. Wood, hollow metal, aluminum storefront and narrow-stile doors all take different exit devices and mounting hardware. Existing holes from a knob, lever or deadlatch may not line up with the new device. Door thickness, stile width, frame condition, swing direction and latch location all affect whether the door can be reused cleanly or needs repair before installation.

What is the difference between panic hardware and fire exit hardware?

Panic hardware is built for fast egress on outswinging commercial doors. Fire exit hardware is a specific version used on fire-rated doors and must latch positively after every closing. A fire door cannot usually use dogging to hold the latch retracted, because the assembly has to latch during a fire. The label on the door, frame and hardware matters, and the installed device should match the rated opening.

Can panic bars work with card access or an electric strike?

Yes, but the parts have to be chosen as a system. Some openings use electrified latch retraction, some use an electric strike, and some use a request-to-exit switch tied into access control. The exit side still has to allow immediate egress without a key, code or credential. Power transfer, wire routing, fail-safe versus fail-secure behavior, and fire alarm interface can all change the correct setup.

What causes panic bar installations to fail inspection?

Common problems include mounting the device at the wrong height, using non-rated hardware on a rated door, poor latch engagement, blocked egress, missing closer control, or adding locks that interfere with single-motion exit. Double doors can fail when rods, mullions or coordinators are wrong. ADA egress and NFPA 101 requirements also matter. A proper installation is not just attaching the bar; the full opening has to function correctly.
