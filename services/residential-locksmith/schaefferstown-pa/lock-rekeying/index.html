What lock rekeying actually involves

Lock rekeying starts with the cylinder, not the door. Inside a typical residential lock are pin stacks that match a specific key cut; when those cuts change, the old key stops working even though the handle, deadbolt, and exterior hardware stay in place. On Schaefferstown's historic stone homes and farmhouses, that matters because many doors still use sturdy, older-style hardware that can be reused if the cylinder is sound. I'll usually inspect the brand and model first — Kwikset, Schlage, Baldwin, Yale, or higher-security options like Medeco and Mul-T-Lock — then remove the cylinder, read the existing pinning, and set it up for a new key. If the lock is worn, sticky, or damaged from years of use, a rekey may still be possible, but the parts have to be checked carefully before anything is pinned back together.

The process is straightforward but precise. First comes confirmation that the hardware can accept a rekey rather than a full replacement. Next, the lock is decoded or measured, old bottom pins and master pins are removed, and new pins are matched to the new key. If you have a smart/proximity key system, a high-security deadbolt, or a restricted keyway, the procedure can change and may require different tooling. What makes the job easier is having every key you want changed, along with clear access to the doors. A house near the historic town square, along Route 419, or out by Route 897 may have a mix of old and newer locks, so I check each one individually instead of assuming they all take the same keying scheme.

Good to know

Common questions.

What does lock rekeying actually change?

Rekeying changes the pin combination inside the cylinder so an old key no longer works. The exterior lock, handles, and deadbolt usually stay on the door. It's a practical choice when the hardware still operates well but you want to control who has access after a move, a key loss, or a change in occupancy.

Can you rekey older locks in a Schaefferstown stone house?

Often, yes. Older homes around the historic town square may have solid hardware that can still be serviced if the cylinder and keyway are in good shape. The tech checks the brand, wear, corrosion, and whether the lock uses standard pin tumblers or a less common high-security setup before deciding.

How is rekeying different from replacing a lock?

Rekeying keeps the existing lock body and changes the internal pinning. Replacement means installing a new lock set. Rekeying is useful when the hardware is still strong and you just need new keys. Replacement makes more sense if the lock is damaged, stripped, badly corroded, or no longer fits the door correctly.

What should I have ready before you come out to rekey my doors?

Have the doors unlocked if possible, gather every key you want changed, and know which locks need to match or stay separate. If the home has a mix of front, back, and side entries, it helps to label them. On farmhouses and older homes, clear access to the interior side of the locks can make the work smoother.

Do you rekey different brands like Kwikset, Schlage, and Baldwin?

Yes, when the hardware is a standard rekeyable residential lock and the parts are in usable condition. Kwikset, Schlage, Baldwin, Yale, and some higher-security systems like Medeco or Mul-T-Lock all have their own cylinder designs and keying rules. The process depends on the brand, keyway, and whether the lock is standard or high-security.
