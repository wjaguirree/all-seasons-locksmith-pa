What lock repair actually involves

Most people think a sticky or loose lock means the whole lock is worn out. Often, it doesn't. In a lot of Paxtang homes, the problem is a door that's shifted just enough to throw the latch off, a strike plate that's no longer aligned, or a cylinder that's collecting grit from years of use. On the tree-lined blocks near Paxtang Park and along Derry Street, I usually start by checking the door edges, hinges, latch travel, and how the bolt meets the frame before touching the lock itself.

The repair process is practical and careful. First comes a test: key in, key out, deadbolt throw, latch retraction, and whether the door rubs when it closes. Then I look at the hardware brand and build — Kwikset, Schlage, Baldwin, Yale, and higher-security options like Medeco or Mul-T-Lock all repair a little differently. A simple fix may mean tightening screws, resetting the strike, lubricating the cylinder, or repinning worn components with a pinning kit. If the issue is a damaged keyway, broken spring, or miscut key, I may recommend repair or rekeying instead of forcing parts that are already failing. The goal is to get the lock working smoothly without creating new wear.

What makes one repair easier than another is the condition of the door and frame. A solid door with good alignment is usually straightforward; a swollen wood door, a sagging hinge, or old hardware with stripped screws takes more work. Having the key that normally operates the lock helps, and if the lock has been acting up for a while, it helps to mention whether it sticks in cold weather, only on one side of the door, or only when the door is closed.

Good to know

Common questions.

Why does my lock stick even though the key is fine?

A key can be perfectly cut and the lock can still bind because the door or frame is out of alignment. A latch that hits the strike plate, a sagging hinge, or a dry cylinder can all cause sticking. The repair starts with checking the door movement and the hardware fit before assuming the lock itself is bad.

Can a locksmith repair a lock instead of replacing it?

Often, yes. If the body of the lock is intact, many problems can be repaired on site: tightening loose hardware, realigning the strike, lubricating the mechanism, replacing worn springs, or correcting a cylinder issue. Replacement is usually reserved for cracked parts, severe wear, or hardware that can't be made to operate smoothly again.

What kinds of locks can be repaired?

Most residential knobs, levers, deadbolts, and smart-lock cylinders can be repaired or adjusted, including common brands like Kwikset, Schlage, Baldwin, and Yale. Some higher-security locks such as Medeco or Mul-T-Lock may require specific parts or a more careful service approach, especially if the keyway or pins are worn.

What should I have ready before a lock repair visit?

Have the working key, if you have one, and be ready to explain exactly what the lock is doing: sticking, spinning, not latching, or needing extra force. It also helps to note whether the problem happens with the door open or only when it's closed, because that points to either a lock issue or a door alignment issue.
