What lock rekeying actually involves

Lock rekeying is one of those jobs that looks simple from the outside but depends on what is inside the cylinder. If the lock body is sound, the hardware can usually stay where it is while we change the pinning so old keys no longer work and new keys do. That is straightforward on many common residential locks from Kwikset, Schlage, and Yale. The process starts with identifying the lock type, pulling the cylinder, and checking for wear, broken springs, mixed parts, or signs that someone already tried to force the keyway. After that, we match the pins to a new key cut and reassemble the cylinder so it turns smoothly and catches properly on the latch.

What makes rekeying more complicated is not the address; it is the lock itself. Some high-security cylinders from Medeco or Mul-T-Lock need special key control procedures. Smart or keyless hardware is a different task entirely. Older locks may have worn plug chambers, damaged tailpieces, or mismatched cylinders from previous replacements. On vehicles, rekeying is not the same as house locks at all, because modern transponder and smart keys require programming, decoding, or VIN-based key origination rather than simple pin changes. For homes in Dauphin, Cumberland, and Lebanon counties, the best candidates are functioning door locks that still have solid hardware but need the access changed after a move, a key loss, or a tenant turnover.

Before the work starts, it helps to have every key you still have and to point out which doors should match on one key and which should stay separate. That lets us decide whether to rekey each lock individually or pin them alike so the same key works across several doors.

When people call us for this

A homeowner in the Harrisburg area moves into a house with multiple exterior doors and no confidence about who still has keys. Instead of replacing every knob and deadbolt, rekeying lets the cylinders be reset to a new key while the hardware stays in place. We check each lock first because it is common to find a front door deadbolt in good shape, a side entry lock with wear on the keyway, and a back door lock that is not even keyed the same as the rest. A good setup can often be cleaned up into one key system, but worn or damaged cylinders may need replacement instead of rekeying.

Another common call comes after a roommate moves out, a contractor finishes a job, or keys disappear and the homeowner wants access narrowed without changing the look of the doors. In those cases, the process is part lock work and part inventory. We identify which doors use standard residential cylinders, confirm whether they are Kwikset, Schlage, Baldwin, or another profile, then decide whether to rekey, pin alike, or keep certain locks on separate keys. If a deadbolt is sticky, the door is binding, or the latch is not aligned, that can slow the job because the lock needs to work correctly before the new keying can be trusted. Keeping the existing keys, any spare keys, and the door locations organized makes the visit smoother from the start.

Rekeying Variants By Lock Type

Residential rekeying is not one identical job. The simplest version is a standard pin-tumbler rekey on common knobs, levers and deadbolts from Kwikset, Schlage, Baldwin, Yale, Defiant, Weiser and similar hardware. The cylinder is removed, the old bottom pins are dumped, and new pins are matched to a different key cut using a pinning kit and key gauge. If several doors in a Dauphin, Cumberland or Lebanon County home use the same compatible keyway, they can often be keyed alike so one key operates the front door, back door, garage entry and basement walkout.

There are important exceptions. Schlage C keyway hardware cannot be keyed to Kwikset KW1 because the blade profiles are different. Some Baldwin and Yale locks use removable cylinders that rekey cleanly; others require more disassembly from the interior trim. High-security cylinders such as Medeco or Mul-T-Lock are a different category. They may use angled cuts, sidebar systems, telescoping pins or restricted key blanks, so rekeying depends on the cylinder series and key control rules. Those jobs are more exacting than a basic five-pin residential cylinder.

Smart locks also vary. A keypad or Wi-Fi deadbolt still usually has a mechanical override cylinder behind the face, and that cylinder can often be rekeyed like a normal deadbolt. Kwikset SmartKey is its own system: it is not repinned in the traditional way, but reset with a working key and reset tool when the cylinder is functioning correctly. If the lock is worn, jammed or missing the current working key, the rekeying approach changes and may require decoding, cylinder service or replacement of that cylinder only.

Cylinders We Actually Rekey

Residential rekeying is cylinder work. On most homes in Dauphin, Cumberland and Lebanon counties, that means removing the plug from a deadbolt, knob, lever, handleset or garage-entry lock and changing the pin stack so the old key no longer reaches the shear line. Common hardware includes Kwikset KW1 and KW10 keyways, Schlage SC1 and SC4, Yale, Baldwin, Defiant, Dexter, Weiser and residential Arrow cylinders. The parts we touch are small but important: bottom pins, master pins if present, top pins, springs, plug clips, tailpieces, cams and sometimes the cylinder cap or retainer. The latch, bolt and strike are usually left alone unless wear or misalignment is causing the lock to bind.

Not every cylinder rekeys the same way. A standard Schlage or Kwikset pin-tumbler cylinder is handled with pinning kits, plug followers, shims when needed, and key gauges to match the new cut depths. Kwikset SmartKey is different; it uses a resettable wafer-style system rather than conventional pin stacks, and it must be in working order with a functioning key or it may need cylinder service before it can be reset. Higher-security residential hardware such as Medeco, Mul-T-Lock and some restricted keyways may involve sidebars, angled cuts, telescoping pins or authorization-controlled blanks, so the process depends on the exact cylinder and key control rules. Rekeying changes access at the cylinder level; it does not turn Grade 3 hardware into Grade 1 hardware or fix a worn thumbturn, loose escutcheon or sagging door.

Rekeying Step By Step

The technician starts by confirming which doors and locks are supposed to change, then checks that the key you want to use is compatible with the existing hardware. A Schlage key will not operate a Kwikset cylinder, and a Yale, Baldwin, Arrow, or Falcon cylinder may use a different keyway again. Each lock is tested before it is taken apart: deadbolt throw, knob or lever operation, latch alignment, strike fit, thumbturn movement, and whether the door is pulling the bolt out of line. Rekeying fixes key control; it does not fix a warped door, loose hinges, or a deadbolt that has to be forced.

Next the cylinder is removed from the knob, lever, or deadbolt. On many residential locks that means taking off the interior trim, removing the tailpiece or retaining clip, and sliding the plug out with a plug follower so the top pins and springs stay in place. The old bottom pins are emptied, the existing key is decoded or measured with a key gauge, and the cylinder is repinned with the correct sizes from a manufacturer-style pinning kit. If master pins, worn pins, bent springs, or damaged plug chambers are found, the technician has to decide whether they can be corrected safely or whether that cylinder is no longer a good candidate for rekeying.

After the plug is reassembled, every cylinder is tested outside the door before it goes back in. The new key should turn smoothly without lifting, jiggling, or pulling back. Once installed, the technician checks both sides of the door where applicable, verifies the bolt fully extends into the strike, confirms the thumbturn works, and makes sure no old key still operates any rekeyed lock.

Why Rekey Difficulty Changes

A straightforward residential rekey is usually a standard pin-tumbler cylinder in decent shape, with a working key available and hardware that comes apart the way it should. Common Kwikset, Schlage, Baldwin, Yale, Arrow, and Falcon residential cylinders can often be removed cleanly, decoded or matched to a new key, and repinned with the right bottom pins, drivers, and springs. If the door is hung correctly and the deadbolt throws without binding, the lock can be rekeyed and tested without turning the job into a hardware repair. Having all exterior doors available at the same visit also helps, because knob cylinders, deadbolts, garage entry locks, and detached-building locks can be keyed alike or separated by function.

The hard jobs are the ones where the lock still looks normal from the outside but is fighting you inside. A missing working key means the cylinder may need to be picked, shimmed, or decoded before it can be rekeyed. Worn key copies can lie to the gauge, especially when a homeowner has been using a rounded-off duplicate for years. Old master pins left from a prior setup can create ghost keys or sloppy shear lines if they are not found and removed. Some cylinders have seized retaining screws, corroded tailpieces, bent clips, or plugs that want to dump pins the moment they are pulled.

Difficulty also goes up with high-security or restricted keyways such as Medeco or Mul-T-Lock, and with builder-grade hardware that has been painted over, overtightened, or misaligned. In Dauphin, Cumberland, and Lebanon county homes, we see everything from newer SmartKey-style cylinders to older mortise and rim cylinders. Rekeying is still often the right answer, but the lock decides how simple it is, not the label on the door.

Before the Rekey Visit

Before the van is on the way, decide exactly which doors should operate on the new key. Walk the house and count every keyed cylinder: front door deadbolt and knob, back door, garage entry door, basement door, storm door, detached garage, shed, and any interior keyed locks. A deadbolt and a knob on the same door count as two cylinders. If you want one key to work everything, the locks usually need to share a compatible keyway, such as common Kwikset KW1 or Schlage SC1. Mixed brands can sometimes be matched, but not always. A Baldwin lock may use a Schlage-style keyway, or it may not. Older Yale, Arrow, or imported cylinders may require a different plan.

Gather every working key you have, even keys you do not plan to use. Existing keys help identify the keyway and can speed up cylinder removal and testing. If you just bought the home, have proof that you are authorized to change the locks, such as settlement paperwork, a lease, or ID showing the address. In Dauphin, Cumberland, and Lebanon counties, many homes have a mix of original hardware, later replacement deadbolts, and garage service-door locks added years apart, so expect the technician to inspect before promising a single-key result.

Also decide how many new keys you want cut and who should receive them. Point out sticky locks, loose trim, doors that need to be lifted to latch, or keys that only work when jiggled. Rekeying changes the pins inside the cylinder; it does not straighten a warped door, repair a worn latch, or fix a misaligned strike plate unless that work is specifically addressed during the visit.

Good to know

Common questions.

What does lock rekeying actually change?

Rekeying changes the pins inside the lock cylinder so the old key stops working and a new key works. The outer hardware stays on the door. It is a good option when the lock body is solid but you want to control who has access after a move, a lost key, or a change in occupancy.

Is rekeying better than replacing the whole lock?

It depends on the condition of the hardware. If the deadbolt or knob is in good shape, rekeying is usually the cleaner solution because it keeps the existing lock and simply changes the keying. Replacement makes more sense if the cylinder is worn, the mechanism is damaged, or the lock is a lower-grade model that no longer works smoothly.

Can all my house locks be made to use one key?

Often yes, as long as the locks are compatible and in decent working condition. That is called keying alike. We first check the brand and cylinder type, then see whether the locks can be pinned to match one key. Some combinations work easily; others need different cylinders or separate keying.

What information should I have ready before calling for rekeying?

Have the addresses or doors you want changed, any keys you still have, and the lock brands if you know them. It also helps to note whether the door is sticking, the key is hard to turn, or the lock has been picked at or forced. That tells us whether rekeying will be straightforward or whether the hardware needs extra attention.

Do high-security locks like Medeco or Mul-T-Lock get rekeyed the same way?

Not usually. High-security systems often use restricted keyways and special control procedures, so the work is different from standard residential rekeying. The cylinder design, key authorization, and pinning method all matter. We inspect the exact model first and then decide whether it can be rekeyed on site or needs a different approach.

Can different brands be keyed alike during a rekey?

Sometimes. Most residential Kwikset knobs, levers and deadbolts can be keyed alike with other Kwikset-style cylinders. Schlage C-keyway locks can usually be matched to other Schlage C-keyway locks. Kwikset and Schlage cannot normally share the same key because the key blade shape and cylinder design are different. Baldwin, Yale, Defiant and other brands depend on the exact cylinder installed.

What if my house has smart locks or keypad deadbolts?

Many keypad and smart deadbolts still use a standard mechanical backup cylinder, and that cylinder can often be rekeyed like a regular lock. The electronic code programming is separate from the key cylinder work. Some models use removable cores, unusual tailpieces or brand-specific cylinders, so compatibility has to be checked at the door before assuming every lock can match one house key.

Can you rekey a lock if no working key exists?

Yes, in many cases. Without an existing key, the cylinder usually has to be picked, decoded, shimmed or removed so the pins can be reset to a new key. That takes more care than a straight rekey with a working key. Heavily worn cylinders, damaged plugs or seized screws can change the plan, and sometimes replacing the cylinder is the cleaner repair.

When is rekeying not the right choice?

Rekeying changes which key operates the lock, but it does not fix poor hardware. If a deadbolt is loose, the latch is misaligned, the strike plate is weak, or the cylinder is worn enough that keys stick and bind, repair or replacement may be better. Rekeying also will not upgrade an ANSI/BHMA Grade 3 lock into Grade 1 hardware or add high-security key control by itself.
